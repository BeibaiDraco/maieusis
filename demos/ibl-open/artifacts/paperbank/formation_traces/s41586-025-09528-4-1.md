# Question-formation trace

- Review status: `ai_reviewed`

## Starting background

- Perceptual decisions are commonly described as the accumulation of temporally distributed sensory evidence, but behavioral performance alone can be difficult to distinguish from non-integration strategies; the selected Kopec et al. and Hyafil et al. abstracts support the importance of discrete, precisely timed evidence for interpreting accumulation.
- Attractor and recurrent-network theories propose that decision computations may be implemented by population-level neural dynamics, yet the source PaperCase states that the actual neural flow underlying decision formation had not been directly estimated and that existing attractor accounts did not capture the full observed activity.
- The source paper frames decision formation as potentially involving an early input-driven accumulation regime followed by a more autonomous regime associated with commitment.

## Unresolved gap

The provided evidence supports a gap between behavioral or theoretical descriptions of evidence accumulation and commitment and direct population-level characterization of the neural dynamics implementing those phases. In particular, it remained unclear whether a transition from sensory-input-dominated to autonomous dynamics, together with a change in neural mode, corresponds to the animal's internally timed commitment to a choice, rather than merely reflecting movement or another task event.

## Dataset opportunity

The study combines simultaneous population spike recordings during an auditory click-accumulation task with precisely known, randomly timed sensory pulses and intervals without pulses, alongside trial choices. These features permit neural responses to individual inputs to be contrasted with activity during input-free intervals and permit a trial-resolved neural transition to be compared with the later influence of sensory evidence on choice.

Because the sensory input timing is known and the population activity is recorded simultaneously, the paper could estimate input-driven and autonomous components of neural flow without defining the transition solely from behavior. The click-level timing then supplies a behavioral test: if the inferred transition is a commitment event, evidence before it should retain an effect on choice whereas evidence after it should no longer do so.

## Resulting question

What population-level neural dynamics implement perceptual decision-making in rats performing auditory evidence accumulation, and does a transition from sensory-input-dominated dynamics to autonomous dynamics—and the associated change in neural mode—identify the internally timed moment at which the animal commits to a choice?

## Scientific consequence

A transition that predicts the cessation of behavioral influence from later sensory evidence would link a neural dynamical event to decision commitment and support a two-regime account of accumulation followed by autonomous commitment. If later evidence continued to affect choice, or if the transition failed to align with this change in evidence weighting, the transition would not provide the proposed commitment marker and would weaken that mechanistic interpretation. The inferred population flow also bears on whether discrete-attractor, line-attractor, or more flexible recurrent dynamical accounts adequately describe the recorded activity.

The question connects formal theories of decision computation to directly estimated population dynamics and asks whether an internally timed neural event can be behaviorally validated as commitment. Its value is not limited to locating a transition in one task: it provides a concrete way to test mechanistic claims by combining model-free dynamical inference with temporally resolved sensory perturbation or weighting, while preserving an interpretable link between neural state and behavior.
