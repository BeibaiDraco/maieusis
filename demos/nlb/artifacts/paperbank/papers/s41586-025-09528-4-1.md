## Question the system extracted

What dynamical regimes and neural modes underlie perceptual decision-making, and does a transition from sensory-input-driven evidence accumulation to autonomous dynamics mark the moment of decision commitment?

## The scientific contrast

A single attractor-like evidence-accumulation mode versus sequential neural regimes in which an initial input-driven mode is followed by a distinct autonomous, decision-commitment mode.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- A bistable attractor model in which the two attractors represent the two choices.
- A drift-diffusion-model line attractor in which sensory evidence drives a one-dimensional decision variable until it reaches a commitment bound, after which inputs no longer affect the state.
- A recurrent-neural-network-inspired line attractor with non-normal autonomous dynamics and evidence inputs that are not aligned with the attractor.
- A two-regime account in which sensory inputs dominate initially and autonomous dynamics later dominate along a different decision-commitment axis.

What would tell them apart: The competing accounts can be distinguished by estimating input-driven and autonomous flow fields from simultaneous spike trains, examining whether trajectories change direction and neural mode during a trial, and testing whether auditory evidence before but not after the inferred neural commitment time affects the behavioural choice.

## Background and motivation

- Perceptual decision-making is thought to be mediated by neuronal networks with attractor dynamics.
- Attractor theories and experiments have supported a role for attractors in perceptual decisions, but the actual population-level dynamics underlying decision-making had not been directly estimated.
- Existing neural attractor hypotheses include bistable attractors, a drift-diffusion-model line attractor, and a recurrent-neural-network-inspired line attractor with non-normal autonomous dynamics.
- The existing hypotheses did not adequately describe the recorded spiking data and the complex single-neuron temporal profiles observed during decision-making.
- A behavioural drift-diffusion model distinguishes an initial regime in which momentary sensory inputs change a decision variable from a later regime in which the state becomes independent of sensory inputs after reaching a bound.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question distinguishes among mechanistic accounts of perceptual decision-making, identifies how sensory evidence and internal dynamics contribute over time, and tests whether a neural population transition can provide a trial-specific marker of when a decision becomes committed.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Rats listened to randomly timed auditory pulses from the left and right and reported the side with more pulses by turning to that side. Pulsatile evidence allowed neural responses to be sampled both time-locked to pulses and during intervals without pulses.

- Population: Twelve rats performing a perceptual decision task; recordings included hundreds of neurons per session and six frontal cortical and striatal regions.
- Measurements: neural spike trains, population-level latent dynamics, input-driven and autonomous dynamics, decision-relevant neural trajectories, behavioural choices, psychophysical kernels, neural commitment time estimates
