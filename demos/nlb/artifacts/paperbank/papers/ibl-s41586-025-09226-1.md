## Question the system extracted

Where and how is prior information represented and communicated across the mouse brain during probabilistic visual decision-making?

## The scientific contrast

Does prior information appear only in late decision-making areas, or is it represented across cortical and subcortical regions at all levels of processing through bidirectional, loopy communication between areas?

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Prior information is encoded only at late stages in decision-making areas.
- Prior information is distributed throughout the brain, including early sensory areas, as expected from a brain-wide Bayesian network with bidirectional interarea communication.
- Apparent neural prior signals may instead reflect an embodied prior arising from body posture, movement, or eye position.
- Signals attributed to the prior may reflect stimulus identity rather than prior information.

What would tell them apart: Decode the Bayes-optimal prior during periods with minimal stimulus and wheel-movement information, compare its representation across early sensory, motor, associative, and higher-order regions, test directed interarea prior-flow patterns, and control for video-derived motor and eye-position features.

## Background and motivation

- Combining sensory information with prior knowledge through probabilistic inference is crucial for perception and cognition.
- In simple cases, the brain performs inference near-optimally according to Bayesian decision theory, and prior information has been reported in parietal, orbitofrontal, and prefrontal cortical areas.
- A brain-wide Bayesian-network account predicts that probabilistic inference can operate in all directions and that prior information should be available throughout the brain, including low-level sensory areas.
- Prior neural representations are poorly understood, and it remains unclear where and how prior knowledge is represented in the brain.
- Previous studies collectively recorded only a limited set of areas and used different tasks, so their results could not be fully integrated.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question distinguishes competing models of neural Bayesian inference: a localized late-decision implementation versus a distributed network in which prior information is represented and communicated across processing levels. The result determines how prior knowledge should be understood in relation to sensory processing, decision-making, and interarea brain communication.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Mice discriminated whether a visual grating appeared in the left or right visual field. The probability of a right-sided stimulus alternated between 0.2 and 0.8 in uncued blocks of variable length; stimulus contrast varied across trials, including zero-contrast trials.

- Population: Mice performing a visual decision-making task; electrophysiological recordings covered 242 brain regions, with widefield imaging from cortical layers 2/3.
- Measurements: choice behavior and psychometric curves, neural activity during the intertrial interval and after stimulus onset, decoding of the Bayes-optimal prior and stimulus identity, body posture, whisking motion, licking, and eye position
