## Question the system extracted

Do neural populations encode the values of multiple sequential offers in semi-orthogonal subspaces that bind value to offer location or presentation order while preserving abstract value information and generalization, and do those geometries predict behavioral binding and generalization errors?

## The scientific contrast

Whether neural codes should be parallel, fully orthogonal, or semi-orthogonal: parallel codes support abstraction and generalization but risk misbinding, whereas orthogonal codes support binding but impair generalization. The paper tests whether semi-orthogonal codes mediate this trade-off.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Parallel or additive value representations could encode offer values but would not preserve which value occurred at which position when multiple offers are present.
- Fully orthogonal representations could bind values to positions but would make value decoding at one position fail to generalize to another.
- Nonlinear mixed-selectivity or interaction representations could bind value and space, but their geometry and behavioral consequences need not be the semi-orthogonal code predicted by the paper.
- Temporal binding could explain offer identity if first and second offers occupy distinct time-related subspaces, rather than spatially distinct subspaces.

What would tell them apart: A semi-orthogonal code predicts distinct left- and right-offer value subspaces, above-chance decoding across locations and offer order, spatial rather than temporal misbinding errors, and a geometry-dependent trade-off between misbinding and generalization error.

## Background and motivation

- During choice behavior, the brain must link each option's anticipated value with the action used to select it, making action-value binding a fundamental neuroeconomic problem.
- Nonlinear mixed selectivity can support binding and flexible behavior, but assigning each position its own subspace becomes impractical as the number of positions increases.
- Fully orthogonal representations can preserve feature-position distinctions, but they sacrifice abstraction: a value decoder trained at one position would not generalize to another.
- Semi-orthogonal representations occupy an intermediate space between parallel and orthogonal codes and could therefore combine binding with abstraction and generalization.
- Theoretical work and prior observations motivate testing whether neural population geometry can encode multiple offers in distinct but not fully orthogonal subspaces.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question would clarify how neural systems preserve which value belongs to which option without giving up abstract value codes that generalize across locations, and would connect representational geometry to concrete behavioral errors in choice.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

A two-option asynchronous risky gambling task. Offers with randomly chosen probabilities and stakes are presented sequentially on the left and right, followed by simultaneous reappearance and choice by saccade. The task includes offer-presentation and delay periods, allowing comparison of spatial and temporal binding.

- Population: Six rhesus macaques performing the choice task; responses from 929 neurons across five brain regions.
- Measurements: Neural activity from OFC, pgACC, PCC, vmPFC and VS, Offer probability and stakes, Model-derived subjective value, Choice behavior and behavioral errors, Representational geometry and subspace correlations, Value decoding, spatial generalization and temporal generalization
