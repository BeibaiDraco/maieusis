# Question-formation trace

## Starting background

- Prior information is relevant to perceptual decisions, and prior-related signals have been reported in higher-order decision-associated regions as well as in sensory cortex, but the provided paper-local evidence presents these findings as difficult to reconcile across tasks and sampled areas.
- Theoretical and computational work on recurrent inference provides a precedent for interpreting near-Bayesian behavior as arising from distributed representations and interactions rather than from a single late decision variable.
- A brain-wide standardized mouse decision-making dataset offers a way to compare representations across processing levels under a common task, addressing a limitation of studies that used different tasks and limited anatomical coverage.

## Unresolved gap

It remained unclear whether prior information is mainly incorporated in late decision-making regions or is represented across early sensory, motor, associative, cortical, and subcortical areas through recurrent or bidirectional interactions. Earlier evidence was difficult to integrate because studies sampled different areas and tasks, and prior probability could be confounded with reward or embodied behavioral variables.

## Dataset opportunity

The source paper reused recordings from mice performing the same visual decision-making task, combining brain-wide Neuropixels recordings, widefield calcium imaging, common anatomical registration, trial-history-based prior manipulation, variable contrast including zero-contrast trials, and video-derived behavioral measurements.

Because the same task defines a trial-history prior while brain-wide recordings place neural activity on a common anatomical framework, the dataset permits comparison of prior-related representations across processing levels and modalities. Zero-contrast trials provide a condition in which prior information is especially informative for behavior, while video features provide an opportunity to test whether apparent neural prior signals can instead be explained by posture, movement, licking, whisking, or eye position.

## Resulting question

Where and how is information about the likely side of a visual stimulus represented across the mouse brain, and do its distribution and inter-area dynamics better support a distributed Bayesian-network account with recurrent or bidirectional communication than an account in which prior information is incorporated mainly in late decision-making areas?

## Scientific consequence

A predominantly late and localized representation would support models in which prior information is added near the decision stage, whereas representation across early and higher-order areas together with inter-area feedback would support a distributed recurrent implementation of inference. Evidence that neural signals are explained by video-derived behavior would instead favor an embodied or motor-history account over a purely neural prior representation. Thus, both localized and distributed outcomes would constrain how probabilistic inference is implemented, while null or confound-sensitive results would qualify the interpretation of decoded prior signals.

The question connects an unresolved computational issue—how prior knowledge is implemented—with an anatomical issue—where that information is available in the brain. Its value is not limited to locating a correlational signal: comparing spatial distribution, inter-area communication, and behavioral alternatives can distinguish competing mechanistic accounts using a common task and shared anatomical reference. As a PaperBank pattern, it illustrates how secondary analysis of a standardized, broad-coverage dataset can make previously fragmented literature comparisons more discriminating.
