## Starting background

- Action-value binding requires linking an option’s value to the position or action used to select it, while abstract or factorized value representations can support generalization across positions and contexts.
- The selected literature provides relevant precedents for spatially associated value representations, low-dimensional subspaces that preserve ordinal or spatial information, and population-level dimensionality analyses; however, no in-text citation contexts were provided, so their precise role in this paper remains provisional.

## Unresolved gap

The paper appears to target the unresolved tension between binding and generalization: parallel or abstract codes may generalize across positions but risk confusing which value belongs to which offer, whereas fully separated codes may preserve bindings but reduce abstraction and cross-position transfer. It asks whether an intermediate, semi-orthogonal geometry can provide both functions and whether that geometry predicts characteristic behavioral errors.

## Dataset opportunity

The dataset contains neural population recordings during trials in which two risky offers are presented sequentially at left or right locations, with presentation order randomized, followed by simultaneous choice. This design varies spatial position and temporal order while recording offer values, population activity, and choice behavior.

Because position and presentation order are separable in the task, the recordings permit comparison of value representations associated with left versus right offers and with first versus second offers. Population activity can therefore test whether value information occupies partially separated subspaces, while decoding and choice errors can connect that geometry to binding, generalization, and spatial-versus-temporal misbinding.

## Resulting question

Do neural populations in reward-related regions represent the values of spatially distinct and temporally separated offers in semi-orthogonal subspaces that preserve offer-location binding while retaining abstract value information and supporting generalization across positions and times?

## Scientific consequence

Evidence for semi-orthogonal subspaces would support an intermediate coding principle in which partial separation reduces value-location ambiguity without eliminating transferable value information. Evidence for parallel coding would favor abstraction with a greater risk of misbinding, whereas fully orthogonal or primarily temporal coding would imply stronger separation at the cost of reduced cross-position generalization or a different account of how offers are bound. The geometry should consequently predict distinct patterns of decoding generalization and spatial versus temporal choice-related errors.

The question connects a fundamental problem in neuroeconomics—how represented values are transformed into correctly directed choices—to a measurable property of neural population organization. Its value is not limited to identifying a code in these recordings: it shows how a formal trade-off can generate discriminating neural and behavioral predictions, providing a reusable example of linking representational geometry to function.
