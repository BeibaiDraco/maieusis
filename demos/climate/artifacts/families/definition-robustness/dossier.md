# Scientific robustness across vortex-state and event definitions

This is a development planning dossier. It is planning-only and does not report scientific results.
Dataset grounding level: `documentation_inventory_only`.
Dataset claim status: `unverified`.

## Question Family

Asks whether substantive conclusions about polar-vortex variability survive changes in representation. One variant concerns event identity and lifecycle structure; the other concerns the stability of precursor relationships.

## Scientific Motivation

Threshold events, continuous circulation states, and alternative vertical representations may identify different phenomena, so an apparent dynamical conclusion may be robust or may be induced by the chosen definition.

## Competing Explanations And Controls

Competing explanations that must remain visible, by variant: Variant 1: Agreement may be induced by using the same scalar intensity extremum to align both representations rather than by a portable lifecycle. Disagreement may result from unequal persistence, duration, seasonal sampling, or temporal resolution rather than distinct dynamics. Recurrent-state construction may smooth genuinely abrupt threshold crossings. Apparent robustness may mix displacement, stretching, or wave-phase configurations with different lifecycle structures. Weak-to-strong strengthening and strong-to-weak weakening may differ because of asymmetric rates or mechanisms rather than behaving as sign-reversed versions of one process. Variant 2: Apparent cross-representation agreement could arise because nominally different diagnostics are algebraically redundant or share nearly all underlying information. Disagreement among forcing-history constructions could reflect their sensitivity to different stages, heights, or wavenumber components of wave propagation rather than failure of the physical relationship. Disagreement among transition constructions could result from major/minor or split/displacement sample composition rather than precursor-measurement sensitivity. An association with downward behavior or a tropospheric signal could be unstable even when the antecedent association with the stratospheric transition is stable. Magnitude differences could reflect unequal scaling, precision, event prevalence, or duration rather than a substantive reversal of the relationship. Seasonal or background-state differences could generate apparent subclass dependence despite a common underlying forcing–transition process.
