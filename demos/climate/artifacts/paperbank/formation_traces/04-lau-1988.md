## Starting background

- Prior observational work had characterized climatological synoptic-scale variability and its relation to midlatitude storm-track regions, while low-frequency circulation studies had focused more heavily on monthly or seasonal first-moment fields.
- Selected case studies suggested that slowly varying circulation patterns could modulate storm-track intensity and position, but the provided PaperCase describes a need to assess whether such relationships recur across the broader winter record rather than only in selected events.
- The source paper builds on a filtering precedent that isolates several-day synoptic disturbances from gridded atmospheric time series.

## Unresolved gap

It remained unclear how broadly monthly mean circulation anomalies are related to recurrent month-to-month changes in storm-track intensity and position, and whether the associated transient eddies are primarily responding to the slowly varying flow or also systematically forcing it. Case studies of blocking could not establish whether their apparent eddy-forcing geometry was representative of winter circulation variability more generally.

## Dataset opportunity

The NMC analysis archive provides a multiyear sequence of twice-daily gridded atmospheric analyses that can be separated into several-day transient variability and monthly mean circulation, producing repeated monthly storm-track statistics across many winter months.

The combination of subweekly temporal resolution, multiyear coverage, and paired transient and monthly-mean fields makes it possible to identify recurrent spatial modes of storm-track variability, compare them with teleconnection and circulation patterns, and examine whether composite eddy diagnostics align with or diverge from the corresponding mean-flow anomalies.

## Resulting question

Across the winter record, what recurrent modes characterize month-to-month variability in the North Pacific and North Atlantic storm tracks; how are those modes related to monthly mean circulation and teleconnection patterns; and do the associated transient-eddy diagnostics support a primarily passive steering or modulation relationship, an active eddy-forcing contribution to the quasi-stationary flow, or both?

## Scientific consequence

If storm-track modes consistently covary with particular circulation patterns, this would support a general relationship between low- and high-frequency components rather than an explanation limited to selected cases. If eddy forcing is aligned with the mean anomalies across the full record, it would support a systematic interaction between transient disturbances and the quasi-stationary flow; if the displacement seen in blocking cases is not general, blocking would appear to be an exceptional regime. Distinguishing intensity changes from meridional displacement would also clarify which aspects of storm-track variability are associated with different circulation configurations.

The question is scientifically valuable because it connects weather-system variability with persistent circulation structure and tests whether a mechanistic interpretation derived from selected atmospheric episodes extends to recurrent behavior across a broader observational record. As a question-formation pattern, it shows how higher-moment descriptions of a fluctuating system can be made scientifically interpretable by relating them to lower-frequency structure and dynamical diagnostics.
