## Question the system extracted

Do large stratospheric circulation anomalies, identified at the onset of weak or strong polar-vortex events, systematically precede and help predict subsequent tropospheric weather regimes, including AO/NAO behavior, storm-track shifts, and weather extremes; and could the observed delay indicate a causal role for the stratospheric anomalies?

## The scientific contrast

Whether the observed downward evolution reflects a predictive and possibly causal stratospheric influence on later tropospheric regimes, rather than merely downward phase propagation or tropospheric forcing that creates the appearance of downward influence.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Stratospheric circulation anomalies precede and contribute to subsequent tropospheric anomalies.
- The apparent downward influence is an illusion produced by downward phase propagation, while anomalies at lower levels originate from tropospheric waves that modify the stratosphere.
- Stratospheric and tropospheric annular-mode variations are sometimes independent, so any average association may not apply to every event.
- Alternative lower-stratospheric coupling mechanisms may account for the surface response, including induced mean meridional circulation or absorption of reflected Rossby waves.

What would tell them apart: Compare the timing and vertical evolution of large stratospheric events with subsequent 60-day AO/NAO distributions, surface-pressure anomalies, storm tracks, and weather-extreme-related circulation, while evaluating whether the observed lag and independent modeling evidence support more than a coincident downward pattern.

## Background and motivation

- Tropospheric weather patterns change on time scales of a few days, while numerical prediction models have little skill beyond a week; on monthly time scales, predictive skill in some regions comes from El Niño/Southern Oscillation.
- Stratospheric circulation regimes persist for several weeks or more but are generally regarded as having little influence on surface weather, although case studies have shown that large stratospheric circulation anomalies can occasionally reach Earth's surface.
- Upward-propagating planetary waves modify the stratospheric flow and can produce downward phase propagation of zonal-wind anomalies.
- Downward phase propagation alone does not establish that lower-level anomalies originate at upper levels; the stratosphere may instead be modified by waves originating in the troposphere, with altered propagation conditions drawing mean-flow anomalies downward.
- Persistent lower-stratospheric circulation anomalies tend to favor tropospheric anomalies of the same sign, but the mechanism of this coupling is not completely clear and the effect is difficult to quantify.
- The AO and NAO modulate surface-cyclone positions and weather extremes, making changes in their distributions and storm tracks relevant indicators of consequential surface-weather regimes.

## Why this question mattered

If large stratospheric anomalies reliably precede shifts in tropospheric regimes and extremes, the stratosphere could provide predictive information beyond the short skill of numerical weather prediction, potentially extending AO/NAO-related predictability up to two months and improving understanding of weather events with substantial societal impacts. The analysis also bears on whether the stratosphere is merely a marker of tropospheric evolution or may participate in vertical coupling.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Observational composite and regime analysis of large weak- and strong-polar-vortex events, examining the subsequent 60-day evolution of stratospheric and tropospheric circulation.

- Population: Northern Hemisphere winter atmosphere, primarily November to April, spanning 1958–1999; annular-mode calculations use fields north of 20°N.
- Measurements: Daily annular modes independently defined at 26 pressure altitudes from 1000 to 0.316 hPa, Geopotential anomalies on a 2.5° longitude by 2.5° latitude grid, Normalized AO and NAO indices, Surface pressure anomalies, Surface cyclone locations and frequencies, Equatorial 40-hPa winds defining QBO phase
