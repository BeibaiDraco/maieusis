## Question the system extracted

Do large stratospheric circulation anomalies, especially weak and strong polar-vortex events, systematically precede and predict persistent tropospheric circulation regimes and associated Northern Hemisphere weather extremes?

## The scientific contrast

Whether the stratosphere is largely decoupled from surface weather, as generally regarded, or whether sufficiently large and persistent stratospheric anomalies descend and provide predictive information about tropospheric regimes.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Large stratospheric anomalies may genuinely precede and influence subsequent tropospheric anomalies.
- The apparent downward progression may be an illusion produced by downward phase propagation of anomalies whose underlying causes originate in tropospheric planetary waves.
- Stratospheric and tropospheric annular-mode anomalies may occur independently rather than being systematically coupled.
- Tropospheric anomalies may sometimes precede stratospheric anomalies.
- The observed coupling may arise through induced meridional circulation or through absorption of Rossby waves reflected downward from the stratosphere.

What would tell them apart: Compare the timing, persistence, sign, and probability distributions of tropospheric AO/NAO and weather outcomes after objectively defined weak and strong 10-hPa vortex events, while examining whether the anomalies descend from the stratosphere and whether the observed lag is consistent with predictive or causal coupling.

## Background and motivation

- Tropospheric weather patterns change on time scales of a few days, while numerical prediction models have little skill beyond a week; on month-long time scales, predictive skill in some regions comes from El Niño/Southern Oscillation.
- Stratospheric circulation regimes persist for several weeks or more but were generally regarded as having little influence on surface weather, although case studies had shown that large stratospheric circulation anomalies occasionally reach Earth's surface.
- Large stratospheric circulation anomalies can descend to the lowermost stratosphere and are followed by anomalous tropospheric weather regimes, including shifts in the distributions of extreme Arctic and North Atlantic Oscillation values, storm-track location, and local mid-latitude storm likelihood.
- Persistent lower-stratospheric circulation anomalies favor tropospheric anomalies of the same sign, but the coupling mechanism is not completely clear and the effect is difficult to quantify.

## Why this question mattered

Establishing a persistent stratosphere-troposphere relationship could extend useful weather prediction from roughly a week toward one or two months for high-impact Northern Hemisphere winter extremes. It could also clarify whether the stratosphere is dynamically relevant to surface weather and inform understanding of how stratospheric changes associated with volcanic aerosols, solar irradiance, or greenhouse gases may relate to surface climate.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Observational secondary analysis of November-to-April circulation data from 1958-1999, defining weak and strong stratospheric-vortex events and comparing subsequent 60-day tropospheric circulation regimes.

- Population: Northern Hemisphere winter atmospheric circulation, from the surface to approximately 57 km
- Measurements: annular-mode values at 26 pressure altitudes, daily geopotential anomalies, Arctic Oscillation and North Atlantic Oscillation indices, surface pressure anomalies, probability distributions of AO/NAO extremes, surface cyclone tracks, equatorial 40-hPa winds
