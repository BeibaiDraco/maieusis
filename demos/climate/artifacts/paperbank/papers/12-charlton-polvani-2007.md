## The scientific contrast

Vortex displacement versus vortex split sudden warmings, compared in frequency, seasonal and interannual occurrence, stratospheric dynamics, precursors, and tropospheric impact.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- The two event types may be dynamically distinct because they have different synoptic structures, precursors, and stratospheric evolution.
- The two event types may have broadly similar integrated tropospheric impacts despite their different lower- and middle-stratospheric structures.
- Differences in apparent tropospheric response may reflect pre-existing tropospheric anomalies rather than a response caused by the stratospheric event; the Pacific-sector anomaly associated with vortex splits is present before the central event date.
- The commonly used NAM index may fail to discriminate event impacts because the response structures of vortex splits and displacements differ.

What would tell them apart: Compare objectively classified vortex displacements and vortex splits across reanalysis composites of event timing, temperature, zonal wind, wave and momentum fluxes, geopotential height, and NAM or RMS geopotential-height responses.

## Background and motivation

- Stratospheric sudden warmings are a prominent manifestation of dynamical coupling between the stratosphere and troposphere.
- Observational and modeling studies provide evidence that the stratospheric state can influence tropospheric circulation, supporting a view of the atmosphere as a two-way coupled system rather than one in which the stratosphere is only a passive absorber of tropospheric planetary waves.
- The influence of sudden stratospheric warmings on tropospheric flow can last for many weeks, making accurate representation of stratospheric dynamics and stratosphere-troposphere coupling important for climate-system models.
- Many individual sudden warmings had been documented, but relatively few studies had established a comprehensive climatology of sudden stratospheric warmings.
- Vortex displacement and vortex split warmings have different synoptic structures, and a simple Fourier decomposition is insufficient to identify the two types.
- Numerical-model validation had largely focused on statistics such as time-mean fields, while process-oriented validation of individual coupled-stratosphere processes was emerging as an alternative.
- The two event types show different stratospheric structures and precursors, whereas their integrated hemispheric impacts on the troposphere are broadly similar.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question establishes a more complete empirical basis for understanding sudden-warming dynamics and stratosphere-troposphere coupling, clarifies whether event structure matters for tropospheric influence, and yields dynamical benchmarks for evaluating stratosphere-resolving general circulation models.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Retrospective climatology of major midwinter stratospheric sudden warmings, objective identification and classification into vortex displacement and vortex split events, followed by temporal and composite dynamical comparisons.

- Population: Northern Hemisphere winter seasons from 1 September 1957 to 31 August 2002, covering 45 November–March winter seasons
- Measurements: Zonal mean zonal wind at 60°N and 10 hPa for event identification, 10-hPa and 100-hPa polar-cap temperature anomalies, Zonal wind anomalies as a function of pressure, latitude, and time, Meridional heat flux at 100 hPa, 300-hPa meridional momentum flux, 1000-hPa geopotential height anomalies, NAM index, Event dates, frequencies, seasonal distributions, and event-type ratios
