## Question the system extracted

How do fast upper-level jet-stream winds, defined as daily 200 hPa zonal winds above the 99th percentile, change under climate change, do they respond additively or multiplicatively relative to the average wind, and what physical mechanism explains their response?

## The scientific contrast

Whether climate change shifts the entire upper-level jet-wind distribution additively toward faster winds or increases winds multiplicatively so that the fastest winds accelerate more than the average; and whether the response can be explained by moisture-related moist thermodynamics rather than by temperature changes alone.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- An additive shift of the entire jet-wind distribution, in which fast winds increase at approximately the same rate as the average wind.
- A multiplicative increase in the distribution, in which fast winds increase substantially more than the average and slow winds less than the average.
- A temperature or dry-thermal-wind explanation based on changes in the meridional temperature gradient.
- A moisture-mediated mechanism involving the moist-get-moister response, saturation-specific-humidity gradients, and associated effects on moist thermal wind.
- Latent-heat-release processes, including moist adiabatic adjustment and changes in the meridional dry-entropy gradient, which may oppose one another and contribute differently across pressure levels.

What would tell them apart: Compare percentile-specific fast, average, and slow wind responses to the response expected from a uniform distribution shift, then decompose moist thermal wind into moist-get-moister, latent-heat-release, moist-adiabatic-adjustment, and dry-entropy contributions across models and idealized warming experiments.

## Background and motivation

- Upper-level jet streams steer weather systems, are linked to severe storms, tornadoes, hail, and severe winds, and affect commercial-flight speed, flight paths, and clear-air turbulence.
- When an entire distribution shifts additively, extremes increase at the same rate as the average; when the distribution increases by a fixed percentage, extremes increase faster than the average. Temperature extremes have been associated with the additive response, whereas midlatitude precipitation extremes have shown a multiplicative response.
- Climate change is projected to accelerate average upper-level jet-stream winds through an increased meridional temperature gradient aloft, with projected acceleration in both hemispheres and a weaker Northern Hemisphere response because of Arctic amplification.
- Understanding how fast jet-stream winds respond is important for flight paths, clear-air turbulence, and severe-weather occurrence, while mechanisms for temperature and precipitation extremes have been studied more extensively.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question determines whether climate change will produce disproportionately stronger record-breaking jet-stream winds rather than merely shift the whole distribution. Establishing a physical mechanism increases confidence in the projections and helps interpret consequences for commercial flight times, clear-air turbulence, and potential severe-weather changes.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Secondary analysis of climate-model projections and reanalysis data. Fast upper-level jet-stream winds are defined as daily 200 hPa zonal winds exceeding the 99th percentile. The analysis compares 1980–2000 with 2080–2100 under SSP5-8.5 and examines near-term trends under SSP2-4.5; it also compares results across a hierarchy of climate-model physical complexity.

- Population: Global atmosphere, analyzed across the extratropics of both hemispheres, primarily 20–60° latitude
- Measurements: 99th-percentile fast 200 hPa zonal wind, Average and percentile-specific jet-stream wind responses, Thermal wind and moist thermal wind, Temperature, saturation specific humidity, dry entropy, and latent-heat-release contributions, Latitudinal structure and satellite-era trends
