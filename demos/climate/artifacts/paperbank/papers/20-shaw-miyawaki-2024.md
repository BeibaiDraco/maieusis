## Question the system extracted

How do fast (>99th percentile) upper-level jet-stream winds change under climate change, do they respond like the average wind or differently, and what physical mechanism explains their response?

## The scientific contrast

Whether the upper-tail wind distribution undergoes an additive shift toward faster winds, with extremes changing at approximately the average rate, or a multiplicative increase, with fast winds increasing faster than the average; and whether the response is explained by thermal-wind changes generally or specifically by moist thermodynamic processes.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- An additive shift of the entire wind distribution toward faster winds, which would produce a fast-wind response comparable to the average response.
- A multiplicative increase in the distribution, which would make fast winds increase substantially more than average winds.
- A response associated with changes in the meridional temperature gradient and dry thermal wind alone.
- A response driven by moisture-related processes, including the moist-get-moister increase in the meridional saturation-specific-humidity gradient and, potentially, latent-heat-release processes such as moist adiabatic adjustment.

What would tell them apart: Compare the climate-change response across wind percentiles with the average-wind response, and decompose the response using thermal wind and moist thermal wind into moist-get-moister, latent-heat-release and related dry-entropy contributions.

## Background and motivation

- Upper-level jet streams steer weather systems, are linked to severe storms, tornadoes, hail and severe winds, and affect commercial-flight speed, flight paths and clear-air turbulence.
- Climate change is projected to accelerate average upper-level jet-stream winds through an increase in the meridional temperature gradient aloft, but the Northern Hemisphere response is weakened by the opposing influence of Arctic amplification, primarily in winter.
- For climate-change extremes, a distribution that shifts uniformly produces an additive extreme response comparable to the average response, whereas a distribution that increases by a fixed percentage produces a multiplicative response in which extremes increase faster than the average.
- Climate-change responses and mechanisms have been substantially studied for temperature and precipitation extremes, but the response and controlling mechanism of fast upper-level jet-stream winds remain poorly understood.
- Understanding the mechanisms controlling climate-change responses is essential for confidence in climate-change projections.
- Average upper-level jet-stream winds are projected to accelerate under climate change, while the behavior of fast upper-tail winds is little known.
- Climate-change extremes can respond additively, at the average rate, or multiplicatively, faster than the average; the applicable response for fast jet-stream winds had not been determined.
- No mechanism had been proposed for the response of fast jet-stream winds, although mechanism understanding is important for confidence in climate projections.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question establishes how climate change affects record-breaking upper-level winds and supplies a physical basis for confidence in projections. It also informs interpretation of projected changes in commercial flight times, clear-air turbulence and possible severe-weather occurrence.

Determining whether fast winds intensify more than average winds establishes how climate change will alter record-breaking jet-stream winds rather than only mean circulation. Identifying the physical mechanism increases confidence in projections and provides a basis for understanding consequences for commercial flight times, clear-air turbulence and severe-weather occurrence.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Quantification of climate-change responses in fast upper-level jet-stream winds, defined as daily 200 hPa zonal winds exceeding the 99th percentile, comparing historical and future periods under emissions scenarios and evaluating the response across a hierarchy of climate-model complexity.

- Population: Extratropical upper-level jet streams in both hemispheres, with comparisons to the satellite-era atmosphere
- Measurements: fast (>99th percentile) 200 hPa zonal wind, average and percentile-dependent jet-stream wind response, thermal wind, moist thermal wind, temperature and saturation-specific-humidity gradients, latent-heat-release contributions, linear trends and signal emergence
