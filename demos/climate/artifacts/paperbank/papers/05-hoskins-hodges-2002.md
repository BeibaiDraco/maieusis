## The scientific contrast

A grid-point Eulerian description of synoptic-timescale activity versus a system-centered description of tracked cyclonic and anticyclonic features, compared across meteorological variables and vertical levels.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Storm-track activity may be adequately represented by broad Eulerian variance patterns, or system-centered tracking may reveal additional organization and attributes not available from variance alone.

## Background and motivation

- Storm tracks have traditionally been diagnosed either by identifying and tracking weather systems or by calculating grid-point statistics such as variance in a synoptic-timescale frequency band.
- Eulerian methods provide a general storm-track activity measure but do not reveal all properties of the systems making up that activity, and most storm attributes can only be inferred from such statistics.
- Objective automated feature tracking can provide complementary information based on the synoptic systems themselves and can distinguish activity by system type, including cyclonic and anticyclonic systems.
- Earlier feature-tracking applications generally used a restricted set of fields, while those fields have limitations: mean sea level pressure and geopotential height are strongly influenced by large spatial scales, whereas vorticity better identifies smaller-scale systems and earlier stages of their life cycles but can be noisy.
- Different meteorological fields emphasize different synoptic feature scales, and the potential-temperature field on the PV = 2 PVU surface provides an additional upper-tropospheric perspective in this context.

## Why this question mattered

Answering the question would clarify what the major Northern Hemisphere winter storm tracks consist of, how cyclonic and anticyclonic systems differ, where systems originate and decay, and whether upper-tropospheric organization helps explain lower-tropospheric storm-track activity. It would also show which aspects of storm-track structure are visible in standard Eulerian diagnostics and which require system-centered analysis.
