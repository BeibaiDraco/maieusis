## Question the system extracted

Can internal multi-decadal variability, as represented in climate-model initial-condition large ensembles, explain the mismatch between observed and modeled SST and SLP trends over 1979–2020, or do the discrepancies indicate systematic biases in the modeled transient response to external forcing?

## The scientific contrast

Anomalous internal variability versus systematic model bias in the forced climate response.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- The observed-model differences result from an anomalous realization of internal multi-decadal atmosphere–ocean variability.
- The differences result partly from systematic model biases in the transient response to historical anthropogenic forcing.
- The discrepancies reflect model biases in the amplitude or spatial pattern of multi-decadal variability rather than only biases in the forced response.
- For the Southern Ocean, anomalous Southern Ocean multi-decadal variability remains plausible, while biased or missing wind, ozone, greenhouse-gas, precipitation, or ice-sheet-melt effects could also contribute.

What would tell them apart: If the observed trends lie in the tails of the distributions produced by model internal variability, especially for patterns and regions with weak modeled multi-decadal variability, then internal variability alone is unlikely to explain the discrepancy; persistent observed-model differences would support a contribution from model biases in the forced response.

## Background and motivation

- Regional climate change depends on the spatial pattern of warming as well as the magnitude of global warming, because SST patterns influence atmospheric circulation and regional climate.
- Observed surface-temperature trends since 1979 show intensified warming in the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool, slight cooling in the eastern equatorial Pacific, and cooling in the Southern Ocean.
- State-of-the-art coupled climate models generally project enhanced eastern-equatorial-Pacific warming, Walker-circulation weakening, and Southern Ocean warming, in contrast to the observed recent trends.
- Sea-level-pressure observations indicate that the Walker circulation strengthened since 1979, whereas climate-model hindcasts do not reproduce this strengthening.
- The relative rate of Indo-Pacific Warm Pool warming is particularly anomalous in observations compared with models, and the southwest Pacific also exhibits a large observed-model discrepancy not previously identified in the cited discussion.
- The observed SST and SLP trends over 1979–2020 are highly anomalous in several regions and indices compared with the combination of forced response and internal variability represented by a multi-model large ensemble.
- The relative Indo-Pacific Warm Pool warming rate shows little multi-decadal variability in CMIP models, making the large observed-model difference difficult to explain by internal variability alone.

## Why this question mattered

Determining whether recent observed trends reflect internal variability or systematic model bias affects attribution of historical climate change, interpretation of regional circulation and precipitation impacts, and confidence in multi-decadal projections. The paper states that without understanding the anomalous trends or the timing and magnitude of delayed warming in the East Pacific and Southern Ocean, a major source of uncertainty remains in regional and global climate projections.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Comparison of observed annual-mean SST and SLP trends with trends from 16 initial-condition large ensembles, using ensemble spread to assess internal variability and a signal-to-noise-maximizing pattern analysis to isolate observed changes not reproduced by models.

- Population: The observed climate system over 1979–2020 and simulations from 16 coupled climate models, each with at least 10 ensemble members.
- Measurements: SST trends, SLP trends, Pacific SST gradient, Walker circulation strength, relative Indo-Pacific Warm Pool warming rate, Southern Ocean SST, signal-to-noise-maximizing SST/SLP patterns
