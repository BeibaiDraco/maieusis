## Question the system extracted

Does the brain solve the value-action binding problem by encoding different offer values in semi-orthogonal neural population subspaces, thereby binding values to their spatial or temporal locations while preserving abstract value information and generalization?

## The scientific contrast

Whether neural codes are parallel or fully orthogonal, versus semi-orthogonal codes that can support both binding and abstraction/generalization.

Competing explanations the paper weighed:
- Parallel or additive value-position representations may preserve abstraction but fail to specify which value occurred at which position when multiple offers are present.
- Fully orthogonal representations may bind values to positions but sacrifice abstraction and make transfer of a value decoder across positions difficult.
- Nonlinear mixed-selectivity or interaction representations may bind value and position through conjunctive coding rather than through semi-orthogonal population subspaces.
- Temporal binding could account for offer identity if first and second offers occupy distinct representations, whereas the proposed account predicts a stronger spatial than temporal misbinding signature.

What would tell them apart: The proposed mechanism predicts distinct but non-orthogonal subspaces for left and right offer values, orthogonal subspaces for first and second offers during the post-presentation delay, behavioral errors associated with spatial but not temporal neural misbinding, and a trade-off between binding and cross-location generalization.

## Background and motivation

- During choice behavior, anticipated option values must be linked to the actions used to select those options.
- The action-value binding problem is fundamental to neuroeconomics, but the neural mechanisms underlying value-action binding remain unresolved.
- Nonlinear mixed selectivity can support flexible behavior and reliable representations, but coding each position in its own subspace becomes impractical when many distinct positions must be represented.
- Fully orthogonal representations preserve value-position distinctions but sacrifice abstraction: a value decoder trained at one position would not generalize to another position.
- Semi-orthogonal representations combine aspects of parallel and orthogonal representations and may provide an intermediate coding scheme.
- Choice requires linking anticipated option values to the actions used to select those options.
- Existing coding possibilities create a binding-generalization tension: parallel representations preserve abstraction but cannot specify which value belongs to which position, whereas fully orthogonal representations preserve distinctions but sacrifice generalization.
- Semi-orthogonal representations are described as a possible middle ground between parallel and orthogonal codes.

## Why this question mattered

The question is scientifically valuable because it addresses how neural systems link value to action during choice while retaining representations that can generalize across locations and contexts. A supported answer would provide a mechanistic account of a fundamental neuroeconomic binding problem and connect neural representational geometry to observable choice errors.

Answering the question would identify a candidate neural mechanism for linking anticipated values to the actions or locations needed to select them, while explaining how the brain can maintain that binding without losing abstract value information and transfer across contexts or positions.

## What the dataset offered (as the paper used it)

Two-option asynchronous risky gambling task. On each trial, two risky offers were presented sequentially on the left and right, followed by simultaneous reappearance and a saccade choice. Offer probability and stakes were randomized, and presentation order was randomized.

- Population: Six rhesus macaques; 929 neurons recorded across five reward-sensitive brain regions: OFC, pgACC, PCC, vmPFC and VS.
- Measurements: Neural population activity during offer presentation and delay periods, Offer value derived from session-specific subjective probability and utility functions, Offer location and presentation order, Choice behavior and behavioral errors, Representational geometry, subspace correlations, decoding, binding errors and generalization errors
