# Question-formation trace

- Review status: `ai_reviewed`

## Starting background

- Perceptual decision-making has been modeled as arising from attractor or evidence-integration dynamics, but the source paper states that the population-level neural dynamics underlying decisions had not been directly estimated.
- The local literature includes mechanistic accounts in which sensory evidence is integrated along an attractor-like or low-dimensional decision variable, as well as work showing discrete or nonlinear decision-related neural trajectories.
- The provided abstract evidence for temporal-integration work supports the use of precisely timed discrete sensory samples to characterize how evidence contributes over time.

## Unresolved gap

The prior theories make different predictions about the organization of decision-related population dynamics, but it remained unclear whether perceptual decisions are implemented by one attractor-like accumulation mode or by sequential regimes in which sensory-input-driven accumulation gives way to a distinct autonomous mode associated with commitment. Existing hypotheses also did not adequately describe the recorded spiking data and complex temporal profiles.

## Dataset opportunity

The study combines simultaneous recordings from many neurons with a task delivering randomly timed, precisely known auditory pulses and recording behavioural choices. The pulse timing provides identifiable sensory inputs and intervals between inputs, while variable evidence sequences allow neural trajectories and the influence of evidence at different times to be examined within trials.

Because the sensory inputs are known at precise times, population activity can be analyzed as a combination of input-driven and autonomous dynamics rather than treated as an undifferentiated trajectory. Trial-level behavioural choices then provide an external test of whether an inferred transition marks the point after which subsequent sensory evidence no longer changes the choice.

## Resulting question

Do perceptual decisions exhibit a transition from an initial sensory-input-driven evidence-accumulation regime to a distinct autonomous neural mode, and does that transition provide a trial-specific marker of decision commitment rather than reflecting a single attractor-like accumulation mode?

## Scientific consequence

Evidence for one stable accumulation mode would support a simpler attractor or integrator account. Evidence for a transition to autonomous dynamics, together with a loss of behavioural influence from post-transition evidence, would support a sequential mechanistic account in which decision formation and commitment involve distinct neural regimes. Either outcome would constrain how sensory evidence, recurrent population dynamics, and commitment are related.

The question links abstract attractor theories to directly estimated population dynamics and connects a neural transition to a behavioural criterion for commitment. It is scientifically valuable because it can distinguish mechanistic explanations that may make similar choice-level predictions while differing about when and how sensory information ceases to control a decision. The provided evidence also presents this connection as a step toward a more unified account of perceptual decision-making, although the trace remains a draft for expert review.
