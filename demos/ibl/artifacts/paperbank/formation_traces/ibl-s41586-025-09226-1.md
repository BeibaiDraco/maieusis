## Starting background

- Prior knowledge is an important component of perceptual decision-making, but the neural locus and implementation of prior information remain unsettled.
- The selected literature presents evidence for both higher-level decision-related representations and prior-related modulation in sensory cortex: Forstmann describes probabilistic-cue effects in orbitofrontal, hippocampal and striatal circuitry, whereas Park reports prior-dependent changes in visual-cortex population activity.
- A mouse study by Ishizu and colleagues found prior values across all recorded cortical regions, providing a close precedent for asking whether prior information is distributed beyond a single decision area.
- Theoretical and modeling work on recurrent networks performing hierarchical inference supports the possibility that belief-related information is implemented through interacting, recurrent population dynamics rather than at one isolated processing stage.

## Unresolved gap

The provided evidence supports an unresolved spatial and mechanistic tension: prior information might be incorporated mainly in late decision-making circuitry, or it might be represented across sensory, associative, motor and subcortical regions through recurrent communication. Earlier empirical findings were difficult to integrate because studies sampled different, limited sets of areas and used different tasks. In addition, the uncued block design means that the animal's subjective estimate of the prior must be distinguished from the experimenter's true block prior, and apparent neural prior signals must be separated from stimulus, movement and eye-position variables.

## Dataset opportunity

The source paper analyzed a standardized visual decision-making task with uncued blockwise changes in stimulus-side probability, including zero-contrast trials, together with brain-wide Neuropixels recordings, widefield cortical imaging, common anatomical registration and video-derived behavioral features. The recordings provide cross-region coverage, while the task structure provides a controlled distinction between prior information and momentary sensory evidence.

Because zero-contrast trials remove informative stimulus evidence and the block structure induces a trial-history-based subjective prior, prior-related choice and neural activity can be examined separately from stimulus identity. Brain-wide multimodal recordings then make it possible to compare prior-related representations across processing levels and to examine temporal relationships between regions, while video features provide evidence for testing whether apparent prior signals can be explained by embodied motor or eye-position changes.

## Resulting question

Where and how is information about the likely side of a visual stimulus represented across the mouse brain: is it restricted mainly to late decision-making areas, or is it distributed across sensory, associative, motor and subcortical regions and communicated through inter-area interactions?

## Scientific consequence

Evidence concentrated in late decision-related regions would support a more restricted decision-stage account of Bayesian inference. Prior-related signals in early sensory as well as associative, motor and subcortical regions, especially when detectable during the intertrial interval and on zero-contrast trials, would support a distributed account. Evidence for inter-area feedback would further motivate a recurrent brain-wide network interpretation, whereas attenuation after accounting for video-derived features would favor an embodied or task-variable explanation over a distinct neural prior representation. Thus, both localized and distributed outcomes would constrain different mechanistic accounts.

The question links a basic computational issue—how organisms combine prior knowledge with uncertain sensory evidence—to the organization of neural computation across the brain. It is scientifically valuable because a common task and broad anatomical coverage address limitations that make results from narrowly sampled, task-specific studies difficult to compare. As a question-formation pattern, it shows how a controlled latent variable can be used to test competing theories about the spatial scale and communication structure of a computation.
